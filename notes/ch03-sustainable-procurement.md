## Part A — Sustainable procurement and business performance

### 1. Definition ⭐⭐⭐
**Sustainable procurement** is the process by which organisations buy goods, services and works in a way that achieves **value for money on a whole-life basis** while generating benefits for the organisation, society and the economy and minimising damage to the environment (UK Sustainable Procurement Task Force, 2006; ISO 20400:2017 "Sustainable procurement — guidance"). In the course's words: the focus changes from **best cost** to **best value (economic + environmental + social)**.

### 2. Supplier A vs Supplier B — the opening debate 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
| Supplier A (traditional choice) | Supplier B (sustainable choice) |
|---|---|
| Lowest cost | 12% more expensive |
| High emissions | Renewable energy |
| Child-labour allegations | Zero human-rights issues |
| | ISO 14001 certified |

| Traditional procurement | Modern sustainable procurement |
|---|---|
| Cheapest supplier wins | Ethical supplier wins |
| Faster delivery | Lower emissions |
| Maximum production | Circular production |
| Acceptable quality at inspection | **Quality at source** |

A model answer chooses **Supplier B**: the 12% premium is the visible cost, but Supplier A carries hidden costs — reputational damage (Nike's labour scandals), legal exposure under due-diligence laws (EU CSDDD), carbon costs (CBAM, Scope 3 targets) and supply disruption if the supplier is sanctioned. Use a TCO or weighted model to show B's total value is higher ([P1 and P7](p1-supplier-selection-numericals.md)).

### 3. Why procurement matters 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
*"Procurement is not just about buying. It drives business value across the entire value chain."* Procurement contributes:
1. **50–80% of manufacturing costs** — purchased goods and services typically account for 50–80% of total manufacturing cost.
2. **A major share of Scope 3 emissions** — purchased goods and services usually contribute the highest share of Scope 3 (Category 1).
3. **Supplier labour practices** — procurement influences working conditions, fair wages, health and safety across the supply base.
4. **Product quality** — supplier capability, materials and processes directly shape product quality and reliability.
5. **Brand reputation** — supplier misconduct or poor performance damages brand trust and stakeholder confidence.
Examples on the slide: **Apple, Nike, Toyota, Unilever** depend heavily on supplier performance for quality, innovation, sustainability and brand trust. **"Strategic procurement = resilient supply chain + sustainable growth."** Procurement connects raw-material suppliers, design, packaging suppliers, manufacturer, logistics, retailer and end customer.

### 4. Why companies adopt sustainable procurement — six drivers with examples 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
| Driver | Example 1 | Example 2 |
|---|---|---|
| **Customer expectations** | Apple: recycled and rare-earth materials | IKEA: wood from responsibly managed (FSC-certified) forests |
| **ESG investing** | Unilever attracts ESG investors through regenerative agriculture | Microsoft secures investor confidence through supplier carbon-emission reporting |
| **Regulations** | EU **CSDDD** (Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive) | EU **CBAM** for steel, cement, aluminium, fertilisers, hydrogen and electricity exporters |
| **Climate change** | Nestlé works with farmers on regenerative agriculture to reduce emissions | Toyota encourages suppliers to raise energy efficiency and cut GHG |
| **Supply-chain resilience** | Tata Motors localised ECU semiconductors after COVID-19 | Starbucks invests in climate-resilient coffee farming and supplier support |
| **Brand reputation / risk reduction** | Patagonia audits suppliers and promotes Fair Trade Certified™ production; Nike strengthened supplier monitoring after labour-practice charges | Intel performs conflict-mineral due diligence; Walmart's Project Gigaton works with suppliers to cut emissions |

### 5. Benefits to business performance 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
| Financial | Operational | Strategic |
|---|---|---|
| Reduced waste | Better supplier reliability | Brand trust |
| Energy savings | Higher quality | Investor confidence |
| Lower life-cycle cost | Innovation | (and multimodal transport efficiencies) |

*"Sustainable procurement drives profit, performance and long-term competitive advantage."*

### 6. Cost vs value — from lowest price to lowest TCO 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
| Traditional | Sustainable |
|---|---|
| **Lowest purchase cost** — focus only on the upfront price | **Lowest Total Cost of Ownership (TCO)** — total cost across the life cycle |
| Hidden costs later: higher maintenance, more energy consumption, more waste and disposal cost, shorter product life, environmental and compliance risks | Benefits: lower overall cost, higher efficiency and performance, reduced environmental impact, longer product life, better compliance and risk management |

**Life-cycle cost components**: purchase price → maintenance → waste → energy → end-of-life. *"Look beyond price. Focus on total value. Lowest TCO = higher value + sustainable growth."* ([TCO numerical](p1-supplier-selection-numericals.md))

### 7. Procurement and ESG 📘 ⭐⭐
Procurement must track the same ESG metrics the company reports: environmental (Scope 1–3, carbon intensity, RE %, water intensity, waste recycled %, biodiversity, SBTi alignment, climate risk), social (LTIFR, diversity, human rights in the supply chain, attrition, CSR spending, customer data protection, supply-chain audits), governance (board independence, ESG-linked pay, anti-corruption, whistle-blower cases, compliance incidents). *"ESG = business sustainability scorecard for future business."*

### 10. Challenges in sustainable procurement 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
*"Moving towards sustainability is a journey, not a switch."*
1. **Higher upfront cost** — sustainable materials, technologies and certifications come at a premium; longer paybacks; ROI not always immediate.
2. **Supplier resistance** — change meets reluctance; concerns about cost, capability and complexity; lack of awareness or prioritisation.
3. **Limited data** — no standardised ESG metrics; inconsistent or incomplete supplier data; hard to measure true impact.
4. **Greenwashing** — exaggerated or misleading supplier claims; hard to verify real impact; risk to brand trust and credibility.
5. **Poor visibility** — complex global chains; no end-to-end traceability; hidden risks in Tier-2/Tier-3.
6. **Regulatory uncertainty** — evolving and inconsistent regulations; unclear compliance requirements; future risks and costs hard to predict.
*Overcoming them requires collaboration, transparency, innovation and a long-term commitment to sustainable value.*

**Key takeaway** 📘: *"Sustainable procurement is now strategic, not administrative."*

## Part B — Supplier selection strategies

### 12. Supplier selection criteria: "ESG + RTI" 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
The lecture's scorecard has six families. **"ESG + RTI = future supplier business evaluation scorecard"** (Environmental, Social, Governance + Risk, Technology, Innovation).

| Family | Key areas (measure) |
|---|---|
| **Environmental** | Carbon emissions (tCO₂e), renewable energy %, water management, waste management (solid / liquid / e-waste), circularity %, ISO 14001 certification |
| **Social** | Health & safety practices, training (multi-skill matrix), diversity %, human-rights protection, community engagement, customer data protection |
| **Governance** | Regulatory compliance (# cases), audit compliance (# NCs), transparency (# whistle-blower complaints), cybersecurity & data protection (# incidents), anti-bribery/corruption (% adherence) |
| **Risk** | Business-risk analysis, financial stability (working capital / profitability), geographical location (exposure to natural hazards), supply continuity (# disruptions), speed of adaptation (% agility), cybersecurity (resilience index) |
| **Technology** | Digitalisation (% capability), manufacturing technology (% automation), quality management (# QC tools), traceability systems (RFID, IoT), data-analytics capability (real-time data systems) |
| **Innovation** | R&D capability (# patents), new-product development (# collaborations), continuous improvement (# kaizens), sustainable innovations (# cases) |

### 14. Decision-making tools 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
**Qualitative**: expert judgement, supplier audits.
**Quantitative**:
| Method | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Weighted scoring** | Assign importance weights to criteria, score each supplier, total = Σ weight × score; choose the highest. Simple, transparent; weights are subjective. |
| **AHP** (Analytic Hierarchy Process, Saaty) | Organises the decision as goal → criteria → alternatives; pairwise comparisons on a 1–9 scale give weights; consistency ratio CR < 0.10 checks logic. |
| **TOPSIS** | Ranks alternatives by closeness to the ideal solution and distance from the worst (anti-ideal): normalise → weight → find ideal and anti-ideal → distances S⁺ and S⁻ → Cᵢ = S⁻ ÷ (S⁺ + S⁻). |
| **DEA** (Data Envelopment Analysis) | Measures the relative efficiency of peer units (outputs ÷ inputs) and gives each supplier a DEA score; efficient suppliers form the frontier. |
| **Linear Programming** | Finds the best outcome (minimum cost or maximum value) subject to linear constraints, e.g. order allocation across suppliers with capacity, carbon and quality limits. |

When to use which: weighted scoring for quick, explainable shortlists; AHP when criteria weights are disputed; TOPSIS when you have many quantitative criteria; DEA for benchmarking many similar suppliers; LP for allocating volumes among already-approved suppliers.

### 15. Supplier risk matrix — agri-food example 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
Risk score = **likelihood (1–5) × impact (1–5)**. Likelihood bands: rare (< 10%), unlikely (10–30%), possible (30–60%), most likely (60–80%), certain (> 80%). Impact: insignificant, minor, moderate, major, severe. Bands: **low 1–5, medium 6–14, high 15–25** (the slide prints 15–24).

| Supplier | Risk | Key drivers | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | High | Drought risk, single-region sourcing | Weather monitoring, diversify sourcing |
| B | Medium | Moderate leverage, seasonal cash flow | Financial monitoring, advance payments with controls |
| C | Low | Strong fleet, good on-time performance | Continue KPI monitoring |

### 16. Supplier segmentation and development 📘 ⭐⭐
Segment suppliers (for example with the **Kraljic matrix**: profit impact × supply risk → strategic, leverage, bottleneck, non-critical items) and develop strategic ones through **training, joint innovation, technology transfer, audits, continuous improvement and long-term partnerships**. Development is cheaper than switching when the supplier is strategic or capacity is scarce.

### 17. The way forward: digital procurement and emerging trends 📘 ⭐⭐
**Digital procurement — "technology, transparency, trust"**: AI (smarter decisions, demand prediction, risk mitigation), supplier analytics, blockchain (secure, tamper-proof transactions), traceability (end-to-end visibility), IoT (connected sensors), real-time monitoring of orders, shipments and quality, **Digital Product Passport** (verified product ID, origin, materials, compliance, life cycle).

**Emerging trends — building sustainable and resilient supply chains**: (1) carbon accounting, (2) Scope 3 reporting, (3) **CBAM readiness**, (4) supplier ESG ratings, (5) responsible sourcing, (6) circular procurement (recycled, reusable and renewable materials). *"Sustainable choices today, a better future tomorrow."*

### 18. Case studies 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
**Schneider Electric — Zero Carbon Project for suppliers**
- Target: **50% reduction in the operational carbon footprint of its top 1,000 suppliers** (by 2025).
- Works with key partners that account for **70% of its supply-chain emissions**.
- Three-phase framework: **Analytics, Ambition, Action.**
- *"The road to net zero runs through the supply base."*

**Walmart — Project Gigaton**
- Goal: avoid **1 billion tonnes (a gigaton) of GHG** in its value chain by 2030.
- **Six pillars**: energy, agriculture, waste, packaging, deforestation and product use.
- Suppliers set self-directed, measurable goals on the **Walmart Sustainability Hub**.
- Developed with **WWF** and **Environmental Defense Fund (EDF)**.
- *"Transforming global supply chains into powerful engines of climate action."*

**Asian Paints — ESG-based sustainable procurement**
- Carbon reduction: prioritising alternate grades, new chemistries and lower-carbon suppliers.
- Bio-based goals: raise renewable or bio-based raw-material integration by at least 20%.
- Formulation optimisation: redesign formulations to cut emissions.
- *"ESG-driven procurement begins with selecting the right suppliers."*

**JSW Steel — ESG-based supplier assessment**
- Digital self-assessment: suppliers complete structured scoring on **Synesgy**.
- Action plans and capacity building: training for sustainability awareness.
- ESG compliance embedded in core strategy and supplier selection.
- *"Digital ESG assessments strengthen supplier resilience and transparency."*

### 19. Recap — the five-step logic 📘
**Sustainable procurement → supplier sustainability → decision tools → business performance → competitive advantage.** *"Procurement is no longer about buying products. It is about creating resilient, responsible and competitive value chains."*

## Quick exam answers ⭐⭐⭐
**Q1. Why is procurement central to sustainability?**
It controls 50–80% of manufacturing cost and is usually the largest Scope 3 category, so most of a firm's footprint, labour risk and quality risk enters through purchasing. Choosing and developing suppliers on ESG + RTI criteria lowers lifecycle cost, carbon and reputational risk (Walmart Gigaton, Schneider Zero Carbon).

**Q2. Distinguish lowest price and lowest TCO.**
Lowest price compares only purchase cost; lowest TCO adds maintenance, energy, waste, end-of-life and risk costs over the product's life. A dearer supplier with efficient, durable, compliant products often has a lower TCO.

**Q3. Name five MCDM tools for supplier selection.**
Weighted scoring, AHP, TOPSIS, DEA and linear programming (plus qualitative expert judgement and audits).

**Q4. List the six challenges of sustainable procurement.**
Higher upfront cost, supplier resistance, limited data, greenwashing, poor visibility and regulatory uncertainty.
