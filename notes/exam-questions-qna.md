# Exam Q&A Bank — Short and Long Theory Answers
The end-sem paper (40 marks) mixes numericals with **theory interpretation and application sub-questions**, and **CO1 and CO6 are asked as 3-mark short theory questions** 📘. This bank gives model answers sized for the marks. Numericals are on the five problem pages. Turn on **Self-test** (`T`) to hide answers.

**How to write a 3-mark answer**: one-line definition → 3–4 points or a mini-table → one example (Indian if possible) → one-line conclusion. About 6–8 lines.

## CO1 — SSCM fundamentals and business performance (3 marks each)

**Q1. Define Sustainable Supply Chain Management. How does it differ from traditional SCM?**
SSCM manages material, information and money flows and co-operation across the chain while integrating economic, environmental and social goals from stakeholder requirements (Seuring & Müller 2008). Differences: objective (cost/efficiency → balanced TBL value), horizon (short → long term), flow (linear "cradle to grave" → circular "cradle to cradle"), suppliers (price-based → development-based), metrics (cost, speed, quality → plus carbon, water, waste, human rights, resilience), scope (own facilities → full value chain/Scope 3). Example: ITC eChoupal.

**Q2. Explain the Triple Bottom Line with supply-chain examples.**
Elkington's TBL measures People, Planet and Profit. Environmental: energy, water, packaging waste, fuel (Delhi AQI, Bengaluru floods). Social: labour welfare, supplier ethics, safety, diversity (gig workers, delivery staff). Economic: carbon cost, waste cost, ESG investment, brand value (fuel imports, crop damage). A chain is sustainable only when all three are met together.

**Q3. "Old thinking vs new reality" — explain four shifts.**
Cheapest supplier wins → ethical supplier wins; faster delivery → lower emissions; maximum production → circular production; waste is unavoidable → waste is a design failure. Drivers: groundwater depletion, fast fashion, food waste, EV-battery waste, coal waste.

**Q4. Distinguish cradle-to-grave and cradle-to-cradle.**
Cradle-to-grave covers extraction to disposal, with responsibility ending at end-of-life (linear SCM). Cradle-to-cradle designs products so materials return into new products or safely to nature (circular SSCM), using reverse logistics, remanufacturing and recycling (e.g. Attero/Lohum battery recovery, Renault Refactory).

**Q5. Explain the sustainable supply-chain framework stage by stage.**
Procurement: ethical, local sourcing, supplier audits. Manufacturing: renewable energy, lean, waste minimisation. Logistics: EV fleets, route optimisation, multimodal. Warehousing: natural lighting, smart energy, rooftop solar. Reverse logistics: recycling, returns, circular economy. Together they move SCM from linear to circular.

**Q6. Give four Indian SSCM cases and classify them.**
ITC eChoupal (delivery-based: farmer connectivity, price transparency); HUL sustainable sourcing (delivery-based: regenerative farming); EV-battery circular chain — Tata Autocomp, Attero, Lohum (recovery-based, EPR); Hindustan Zinc–GreenLine LNG/EV trucks and Mahindra carbon neutrality (reduction-based).

**Q7. Define sustainable supply-chain strategy and explain why it matters.**
A long-term plan integrating economic, environmental and social objectives while maintaining competitiveness and profitability. It matters because of regulation (BRSR, CBAM, CSDDD, carbon markets), investors (ESG ratings, cost of capital), customers (audits, green preference) and operational gains (energy, waste, logistics savings). A chain profitable today may be "illegal, expensive or unacceptable tomorrow".

**Q8. What are the risks of ignoring sustainability?**
Export barriers (CBAM, deforestation rules), carbon taxes, supply disruptions (climate, water), reputation damage (labour scandals), investor pressure (divestment, higher cost of capital) and customer loss.

**Q9. Explain Scope 1, 2 and 3 in one line each with examples.**
Scope 1 "what you burn": diesel in DG sets, company vehicles, process and fugitive emissions. Scope 2 "what you buy": purchased electricity, heat, cooling. Scope 3 "what you influence": purchased goods, logistics, product use, end-of-life — usually 70–90%+ of the total.

**Q10. List the five steps of strategy development.**
Assess current state (carbon footprint, network, supplier performance, waste, energy) → analyse stakeholders (who influences, who is impacted, expectations) → develop scenarios (carbon pricing, disruptions, energy cost, water scarcity) → design strategy (green sourcing, RE, circularity, supplier development, network redesign) → operationalise (projects, KPIs, budgets, responsibilities).

**Q11. Explain stakeholder mapping in SSCM.**
Identify internal (employees, management, shareholders) and external stakeholders (customers, suppliers, regulators, communities, NGOs, investors, lenders), their expectations and power/interest; manage high-power/high-interest groups closely. "Successful supply chains don't just deliver products — they deliver value to stakeholders."

**Q12. Draw/describe a Sustainability Balanced Scorecard.**
Financial: energy savings, logistics-cost reduction, waste-reduction savings, ROI of sustainability projects. Customer: sustainable product sales, satisfaction, ESG ratings, supplier compliance. Internal process: carbon emissions, water usage, transport efficiency, SC management. Learning & growth: employee training, sustainability culture, innovation projects.

**Q13. How does SSCM improve business performance?**
Lower cost (energy, waste, fuel), higher revenue and market access (green products, EU eligibility), lower risk and better resilience, lower cost of capital (ESG ratings), stronger brand and talent, innovation in business models (PaaS, remanufacturing). Lecture 10's Company B (energy −25%, CO₂/unit −30%, waste −60%) outperforms Company A's glossy report.

**Q14. Distinguish traditional and sustainable performance measurement.**
Traditional: financial, lagging, short-term, shareholders, firm boundary. Sustainable: TBL plus governance and resilience, leading and lagging, long-term, all stakeholders, whole value chain. "If you cannot measure it, you cannot improve it."

**Q15. What are Q + D + I + R + S performance dimensions?**
Quality (defect PPM, returns, warranty, Six Sigma level), Delivery (OTD, cycle time, lead time, reliability), Innovation (new products, R&D, patents, automation), Resilience (disruptions, recovery time, supplier diversity, continuity), Sustainability (carbon, energy, water, safety, ethical sourcing %).

## CO6 — Technology trade-offs and SSCM strategies (3 marks each)

**Q16. "Technology ≠ sustainability." Explain.**
Technology can cut transport emissions, inventory and waste and improve safety and traceability, but it can raise energy use, e-waste, capital outlay, cyber risk and digital-infrastructure CO₂. The link technology → operational performance → sustainability is not linear; potential ≠ realised benefit.

**Q17. Explain the five hidden trade-offs of technology adoption.**
(1) Efficiency vs environmental footprint (electricity, equipment manufacture, e-waste). (2) Traceability vs energy (blockchain). (3) Visibility vs privacy (cybersecurity, surveillance, data ownership). (4) Global optimisation vs local resilience. (5) Digital inclusion vs supplier exclusion — Tier-1 improves while Tier-2/3 are excluded.

**Q18. Explain the technology–sustainability decision matrix.**
Business value (low/high) × sustainability impact (low/high): low–low Avoid; low value–high impact Reconsider; high value–low impact Optimise; high–high Prioritise. Then score with TAS and NTV.

**Q19. State TAS and Net Technology Value.**
TAS = 25% Economic + 25% Environmental + 20% Social + 15% Resilience + 15% Strategic fit (1–5 each). NTV = Gross benefits − Adoption cost − Lifecycle sustainability cost − Risk cost.

**Q20. What is resilience as the fourth pillar?**
Lecture 9 adds resilience — visibility, agility, recovery, response, assurance — to environmental, social and economic pillars (Carter–Rogers framework: environmentally friendly + socially ethical + economically viable, supported by risk management, transparency, strategy, culture).

**Q21. Describe the Supply Chain 4.0 technology pyramid.**
From the base: ERP/digital integration → automation/robotics/AGVs → cloud/platforms → blockchain → IoT/sensors/RFID → digital twins → advanced analytics/ML → AI/GenAI. Applied across sourcing, production, warehousing, transport, inventory, demand planning, reverse logistics and supplier management.

**Q22. Explain the Walmart–IBM blockchain case.**
Food-contamination tracing took days; Walmart and IBM built a Hyperledger Fabric blockchain with IoT data and QR codes; mango provenance fell from 7 days to 2.2 seconds. Benefits: faster recalls, less waste, safety, consumer trust. Trade-offs: integration cost, supplier capacity, data quality, interoperability, governance, scalability.

**Q23. List the Tech-Sustain 7-step framework.**
Define the problem → establish the baseline (cost, CO₂, waste, service, risk) → identify alternatives → evaluate total lifecycle impact (capex + opex + energy + people + e-waste + cyber) → evaluate the TBL → stress-test unintended consequences → pilot → measure → scale. "Don't digitise the problem — transform it sustainably."

**Q24. Should Navya adopt, pilot or reject Project SUSTAIN_X? (short)**
Pilot. Gross benefits of about ₹46 cr/yr (assumed bases) against ₹12 cr opex, ₹80 cr capex and risk costs give a positive NPV and TAS ≈ 3.8, but NPV turns negative if less than ~87% of benefits arrive, and 1,500 suppliers/8,000 distributors raise inclusion risk. Pilot one plant and a supplier subset with stage-gates, then scale.

**Q25. Explain the polycrisis facing supply chains.**
Climate change, geopolitical fragmentation, critical-resource constraints, regulatory pressure, social risks and technology disruption — simultaneously. The 2030 chain must be green and resilient (WEF Global Risks Report 2026).

**Q26. List the five big sustainability challenges and the strategic shift.**
Carbon (decarbonise the value chain), resources (more with less), circularity (closed loops), people (protect workers and communities), resilience (survive disruption without shifting costs). Shift: efficiency → resilience → sustainability → regeneration.

**Q27. Mitigation vs adaptation in supply chains.**
Mitigation reduces the chain's climate impact (RE, efficiency, modal shift, low-carbon materials). Adaptation reduces vulnerability to climate change (climate-risk site selection, water planning, multi-port and multi-supplier networks, buffers). Best strategy = both.

**Q28. Explain the 7-step strategy architecture.**
Diagnose hotspots → prioritise high-impact/high-risk activities → redesign products, suppliers, processes, logistics, network → collaborate with suppliers, customers, government, competitors, communities → measure carbon, circularity, cost, service, social → innovate (digital, AI, clean tech, new business models) → scale (embed in procurement, investment, governance).

**Q29. "Do not outsource sustainability." Explain.**
Most impacts are in the supply base; a buyer whose price squeezes and erratic orders push suppliers into unsafe or polluting practices cannot claim a sustainable chain. Procurement KPIs must cover total cost, CO₂/unit, RE %, recycled content, water, safety, human-rights assessments, single-source dependency and traceability.

**Q30. "The cleanest kilometre is the kilometre eliminated." Explain.**
Green trucks are not the same as sustainable logistics; first remove km through network redesign, consolidation, better packaging cube, fewer empty miles, sensible delivery frequency and fewer returns, then shift modes and switch fuels.

**Q31. List the eight sustainability transitions.**
Fossil → renewable/electrified energy; virgin → circular materials; linear → circular products; global concentration → resilient diversification; opaque → traceable chains; ownership → access/service models; reactive → predictive/AI management; reporting → sustainability embedded in decisions.

**Q32. What is the critical-minerals paradox?**
The green transition (EVs, batteries, solar, wind, grids) multiplies demand for lithium, copper, nickel, cobalt, graphite and rare earths, whose mining has water, biodiversity, emission, social and geopolitical impacts. IEA 2025: lithium demand +~30% in 2024; 7% of copper supply exposed to flood/drought.

**Q33. Explain JIT + JIC + JIS.**
The old model (global, concentrated, lowest cost, pure JIT) gives way to regional, diversified, digital, circular, low-carbon networks that combine just-in-time efficiency with just-in-case buffers and dual sourcing and just-in-sequence delivery, optimising cost + service + carbon + circularity + risk + social impact for lowest life-cycle cost and risk.

**Q34. What is the strategic lesson of Apple's supply-chain programme?**
Apple treats sustainability as a value-chain transformation (supplier RE, recycled cobalt and rare earths, cleaner semiconductors, disassembly), reaching >60% below 2015 with 17.8 GW of supplier RE and 21.8 Mt avoided in 2024: the OEM orchestrates supplier sustainability.

**Q35. Explain the sustainable supply-chain flywheel and the 7-step roadmap.**
Flywheel: measure → prioritise → redesign → collaborate → digitise → circularise → decarbonise → regenerate → measure again. Roadmap: map → measure → prioritise → redesign → collaborate → digitise → transform (incremental to circular/regenerative models).

**Q36. Why is quality a sustainability issue? (Lecture 10, quiz-relevant)**
Defects → rework → extra materials and energy → extra transport → extra waste → higher CO₂; quality at source prevents waste. Each TIMWOOD waste creates cost + energy + material + carbon + time. Link: TQM + Lean + Six Sigma + green SCM; ISO 9001 + 14001 + 45001 as an integrated system.

## Theory sub-questions for CO2–CO5 (interpretation and application)

**Q37. (CO2) Why should weighted supplier scoring be followed by sensitivity analysis?**
Weights are subjective; if a small change in weights reverses the ranking, the decision is fragile. Sensitivity shows the weight range in which a supplier stays best (e.g. Lecture 6's model: raising quality weight to 30% switches the winner from C to A), and supports negotiation and supplier development targets.

**Q38. (CO2) Compare weighted scoring, AHP and TOPSIS.**
Weighted scoring: simple, transparent; subjective weights; compensatory. AHP: derives weights from pairwise comparisons on a 1–9 scale with a consistency ratio (CR < 0.10); good when criteria importance is disputed. TOPSIS: ranks by closeness to the ideal and distance from the anti-ideal after vector normalisation; handles many quantitative criteria. All are compensatory, so add knock-out rules for human-rights or safety failures.

**Q39. (CO2) Supplier A is 12% cheaper but has child-labour allegations. What do you do?**
Do not select on price. Apply a knock-out/minimum-threshold rule for human rights, compute TCO including reputational and legal risk (CSDDD, customer audits), prefer Supplier B (RE, ISO 14001, zero human-rights issues), and if A is strategic, require a time-bound corrective plan with third-party audits before any award.

**Q40. (CO3) What is MRV and why is it vital for CBAM exporters?**
Measure (activity data: kWh, litres, m³, production), Report (BRSR, CBAM filings, CDP), Verify (internal audit, third-party assurance by bodies like Bureau Veritas, TÜV). Verified embedded emissions avoid punitive default values under CBAM and build investor trust.

**Q41. (CO3) Why can Scope 1 fall while the total footprint rises?**
Firms outsource energy-intensive steps, buy more carbon-intensive inputs or sell more high-use-phase products; Scope 3 rises faster than Scope 1 falls. Hence "carbon accounting must follow the value chain".

**Q42. (CO3) Interpret: a plant's Scope 1 + 2 is 1.1% of its total emissions (ICEV + EV case).**
Plant energy projects alone cannot materially decarbonise the company; the levers are product use (efficiency, EV share, clean grid), purchased materials (green steel, aluminium, battery chemistry) and end-of-life. That is why Toyota and Tesla focus on supplier decarbonisation, RE, battery recycling and green logistics.

**Q43. (CO3) Location-based vs market-based Scope 2?**
Location-based uses the average grid factor of where electricity is consumed; market-based uses the factor of contracts bought (PPAs, RECs, green tariffs). Report both; market-based shows the effect of procurement choices (e.g. Tata Motors' iREC-based Scope 2).

**Q44. (CO4) Why is the cheapest network not necessarily the best sustainable network?**
Cost-minimal networks may have long distances, high CO₂, low recovery and fragile single sources. With a carbon price (λ) or recovery value (μ) in the objective, a dearer network (e.g. Network C, ₹115, 55 CO₂, 70% recovery) becomes optimal above λ ≈ 0.35. Decision-maker priorities and Pareto efficiency define "best".

**Q45. (CO4) Interpret the result that the CO₂-optimal number of DCs is higher than the cost-optimal number.**
More DCs shorten outbound truck-km (less CO₂) but add facility and inventory cost; the gap shows the implicit carbon price needed (e.g. about ₹30,000/t in P7) to justify the extra DC. Managers can close it with green DCs, consolidation or rail on primary legs instead.

**Q46. (CO4) Why is reverse logistics harder than forward, and how do you design the collection network?**
Uncertain quantity, timing and condition, low density, high collection/sorting cost, information gaps, non-standard products, consumer participation. Choose decentralised, centralised or hybrid collection by volume, distance, product value, recovery technology and cost; route each return to the highest-value option (resale → repair → remanufacture → recycle → dispose).

**Q47. (CO4) Explain the centre-of-gravity method and its limitations.**
Cx = Σ xᵢWᵢ ÷ ΣWᵢ and Cy = Σ yᵢWᵢ ÷ ΣWᵢ give the weighted-average location minimising (approximately) ton-distance. Limitations: straight-line distance, ignores roads, land cost, terrain, labour, RE availability and social impact; the CoG point may be infeasible, so compare nearby feasible sites with load-distance and factor rating.

**Q48. (CO5) What is the KPI trap and what makes a good measurement system?**
Too many KPIs (75 sustainability, 40 supplier, 30 logistics) yet no answer to "are we becoming more sustainable?". A good system passes five tests — material, balanced, actionable, comparable, credible — and each KPI answers what happened, why, who owns it, what decision changes.

**Q49. (CO5) Explain leading vs lagging KPIs with SCOR examples.**
Leading: supplier ESG assessments, training, RE contracts, eco-design projects, preventive maintenance. Lagging: supplier incidents, injury rate, Scope 2, product footprint, failures. SCOR + sustainability: Plan carbon-aware planning, Source supplier score, Make energy/unit, Deliver CO₂e/shipment, Return recovery rate, Enable ESG data quality.

**Q50. (CO5) A composite sustainability index is 100.3% "on target". Comment.**
Composites hide failures: over-achievement on cost and carbon can mask safety and recycling misses. Report by perspective, cap achievement ratios, add knock-out thresholds for safety and human rights, and review whether targets are benchmark- or science-based.

**Q51. (CO5) What does BRSR Core change for supply-chain managers?**
It moves from disclosure to assured KPIs across nine ESG attributes (top 150 entities from FY2023–24, rising towards 1,000), so managers need data ownership, internal controls, a measurement methodology, supplier data, audit readiness and evidence trails. "Don't just report sustainability. Prove it."

**Q52. (CO5) Activity vs outcome: "100% of suppliers trained in ESG" — is it proof of improvement?**
No — it is an activity (input/process). Proof is outcomes: supplier emissions per unit down, violations down, audit scores up. Measure the whole chain inputs → processes → outputs → outcomes → impact.

## Long-answer questions (8–10 marks)

**Q53. Design a sustainable supply-chain strategy for an Indian auto-component exporter facing CBAM.**
Diagnose: product carbon footprint (cradle-to-gate, ISO 14067), Scope 1–3 inventory with MRV; hotspots are steel/aluminium inputs and grid power. Prioritise: CBAM-covered products and top customers. Redesign: green steel/aluminium sourcing (scrap-based EAF), RE PPAs and rooftop solar, process efficiency (OEE, heat recovery), packaging and logistics consolidation, rail to port. Collaborate: supplier development and data sharing (Synesgy-style self-assessment), customer co-investment. Measure: kg CO₂e per part, RE %, supplier ESG coverage, OTIF, cost per unit; BSC with leading/lagging KPIs. Innovate: digital product passport-ready traceability, carbon-accounting software. Scale: embed in supplier selection (weighted ESG + RTI scorecard), capex approvals with an internal carbon price, governance. Report via BRSR Core and CBAM declarations; avoid greenwashing.

**Q54. Explain LCA with its four phases and apply it to a PET water bottle.**
Goal and scope: compare PET vs rPET bottles; functional unit "delivering 1 litre of drinking water"; cradle-to-grave. Inventory: resin, energy for blow moulding, filling, transport km, end-of-life split. Impact: GWP, fossil depletion, plastic leakage, water. Interpretation: hotspots are resin and transport (Bisleri), so lightweighting, rPET, solar power and rail cut impacts; sensitivity on recycling rate. Limits: data intensity, allocation, regional variation.

**Q55. Discuss reverse logistics and the circular economy in network design with Indian examples.**
Definition, touch points and condition-based destinations; difficulties; collection configurations; closed-loop architecture; R-strategy hierarchy; circular design; PaaS; nine-node model; strategic/tactical/operational decisions; objective function with returns balance; KPIs (recovery, reuse, remanufacture rates). Examples: Tata Motors Prolife and Re.Wi.Re, Mahindra CERO, Attero/Lohum, Furlenco, JSW-MG BaaS, IKEA buy-back.

**Q56. Critically evaluate technology adoption for sustainability using Lecture 9.**
Technology ≠ sustainability; SC 4.0 landscape and the problem–technology table; five hidden trade-offs; 2×2 matrix; TAS and NTV; Tech-Sustain seven steps; Walmart–IBM and Asian Paints cases; Navya recommendation (pilot); takeaways: enabler not strategy, one KPI can damage another, full life cycle, ecosystems, best ≠ most advanced.

**Q57. Explain the role of sustainable procurement in business performance and supplier selection.**
50–80% of cost and most Scope 3; drivers with examples (Apple, IKEA, Unilever, Microsoft, CSDDD, CBAM, Nestlé, Toyota, Tata Motors, Starbucks, Patagonia, Nike, Intel, Walmart); benefits; cost vs TCO; KPIs; ESG + RTI criteria; tools (weighted, AHP, TOPSIS, DEA, LP); risk matrix; segmentation and development; Schneider, Walmart Gigaton, Asian Paints, JSW; challenges.

**Q58. Explain sustainability reporting frameworks and the risk of greenwashing.**
Why report; CDP, GRI 305, TCFD four pillars, Integrated Reporting six capitals, ISSB S1–S2, BRSR 9 principles and Sections A–C, BRSR Core; GRI = impacts, ISSB = investor risks, BRSR = Indian regulation; Company A vs B; greenwashing vs greenhushing; good reporting = claim, metric, boundary, methodology, baseline, target, evidence; MRV and assurance.
