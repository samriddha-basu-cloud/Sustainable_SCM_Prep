## 1. Green logistics ⭐⭐⭐
**Definition (Lecture 8)** 📘: *Green logistics means designing logistics activities to reduce the environmental impact while maintaining service and economic performance.*

Logistics covers transport, warehousing, inventory, packaging, order processing and reverse flows. Freight transport is one of the hardest sectors to decarbonise: road dominates Indian freight (the IISc paper notes road accounts for about 87% of transport energy; transport is about 22% of India's energy use). Logistics also drives cost: in India logistics cost has been estimated at about 8–14% of GDP (the government's revised estimate is about 7.8–8.9%), against about 8% in developed economies, so greener logistics is usually also cheaper logistics.

**Lecture 11 Strategy 4 — logistics decarbonisation** 📘: *"The cleanest kilometre is the kilometre eliminated."* Order of levers: avoid (fewer km through network redesign and consolidation) → shift (to rail or water) → improve (efficient vehicles, load factor, driving) → switch fuel (CNG/LNG, EV, hydrogen, biofuels).

### The impact of green logistics on business performance ⭐⭐⭐
- **Cost**: fuel is 30–40% of road-freight cost, so fewer km, higher load factor and better fuel efficiency cut cost directly.
- **Service**: route optimisation and consolidation can keep or improve OTIF when planned well.
- **Risk and compliance**: lower exposure to fuel-price volatility, carbon pricing, city low-emission zones and customer Scope 3 audits.
- **Revenue and brand**: customers with Scope 3 targets (Walmart, Apple, Unilever) prefer low-carbon logistics partners.
- **Asset productivity**: better vehicle utilisation and fewer empty km raise revenue per truck.

## 2. The green transportation network ⭐⭐⭐
**Principle** 📘: *use the lowest-impact transport mode compatible with service requirements.*

**Typical hierarchy (emission intensity per tonne-km)**: **AIR > ROAD > RAIL > WATER.** *"Emissions intensity increases towards air freight; actual performance depends on distance, load factor, vehicle and operating conditions."* 📘

| Mode | Typical g CO₂e per tonne-km (indicative) | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air | 500–800 | Speed, high-value and perishable goods | Highest emissions and cost |
| Road (truck) | 60–150 | Door-to-door, flexible, fast for short haul | High emissions per t-km, congestion, safety |
| Rail | 15–30 | Bulk, long haul, low emissions (India's Dedicated Freight Corridors) | Fixed routes, first/last mile by road |
| Inland/coastal water | 10–30 | Very low cost and emissions for bulk | Slow, limited routes |
| Deep-sea container | 5–20 | Lowest per t-km for long haul | Slow; bunker-fuel emissions; port congestion |

### Vehicle and route decisions — the six network decisions 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
| Decision | Example from the slide |
|---|---|
| 1. **Which vehicle?** | Use a 7-tonne truck for regional deliveries |
| 2. **Which route?** | Choose route A (shorter distance and less traffic) |
| 3. **Which depot?** | Ship from the Delhi depot (not Mumbai or Bangalore) |
| 4. **Which delivery frequency?** | Deliver 3 times a week (Mon, Wed, Fri) |
| 5. **Which shipment size?** | Ship in 10-pallet batches |
| 6. **Which customers should be consolidated?** | Consolidate customers A, B and C into one delivery |

Supporting techniques: vehicle-routing optimisation (VRP), milk runs, backhauling to cut empty return km, load planning (weight and cube), eco-driving training, telematics, speed governance, tyre pressure, aerodynamic kits, and alternative fuels (CNG/LNG, EV, hydrogen, biodiesel, ethanol).

### The FMCG distribution example (Lecture 10) 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
10,000 deliveries a month, average distance 180 km, diesel trucks, 62% average vehicle utilisation, 18% empty return km. **Five KPIs**: CO₂e per tonne-km, fuel efficiency (km per litre), vehicle utilisation %, empty km % and OTIF %. *"This is where sustainability becomes a management decision rather than a reporting exercise."* ([Computed baseline and improvement](p3-network-logistics-numericals.md); [SMART targets](p4-performance-measurement-numericals.md))

### Indian logistics decarbonisation cases ⭐⭐
- **Hindustan Zinc + GreenLine Mobility (2025)** 📘: LNG and EV trucks for mineral logistics (₹400 crore deal); reduction-based case with trade-offs between cost, emissions, infrastructure and scalability.
- **Flipkart (2022)** 📘: recyclable packaging, last-mile EVs, route optimisation (Flipkart is part of the Climate Group's EV100).
- **Mahindra** 📘: green logistics as part of the carbon-neutrality goal; Mahindra Logistics' green buildings.

## 4. Green packaging ⭐⭐⭐
| Traditional packaging | Sustainable packaging |
|---|---|
| 1. Large box | 1. **Right-sized** packaging |
| 2. Plastic protection | 2. **Reusable** packaging |
| 3. Pallet | 3. **Recycled** material |
| 4. Stretch film | 4. **Returnable transport packaging** (crates, collapsible bins) |

**Network implication** 📘: *"Packaging is not simply a procurement decision. It affects **cube → vehicle utilisation → number of trips → fuel → CO₂**."* Right-sizing removes shipped air, so more units fit per truck and fewer trips are needed ([numerical](p3-network-logistics-numericals.md)). In India, **EPR for plastic packaging** (Plastic Waste Management Rules) makes brand owners responsible for collecting and recycling their packaging.

## 5. Case: DHL green logistics — six levers 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
*"Delivering today, preserving tomorrow."*
1. **Electric delivery vehicles** — EVs for last-mile deliveries to reduce emissions and air pollution.
2. **Alternative fuels** — bio-CNG, HVO (hydrotreated vegetable oil) and other cleaner fuels.
3. **Multimodal transport** — combining road, rail, air and sea to optimise routes, reduce transit time and lower emissions.
4. **Sustainable warehousing** — energy-efficient buildings, renewable energy, smart systems, responsible resource management.
5. **Shipment consolidation** — maximise load utilisation, reduce trips and lower emissions.
6. **Packaging optimisation** — right-sized, recyclable and lightweight packaging.
*"Smarter logistics. Lower emissions. Greener future."* (DHL's Strategy 2030 targets net-zero logistics emissions by 2050 and more than 66% e-vehicles for last-mile pick-up and delivery by 2030.)

## 6. Reverse logistics ⭐⭐⭐
**Definition (Lecture 8)** 📘: *Reverse logistics manages the movement of products/materials from the customer back toward the supply chain.* It is an important part of the sustainable supply-chain process. (Rogers & Tibben-Lembke: planning, implementing and controlling the efficient flow of materials and information from the point of consumption to the point of origin to recapture value or dispose properly.)

**Why products come back**: commercial returns (e-commerce, wrong size), warranty and repair returns, end-of-use (leasing, trade-ins), end-of-life (scrap vehicles, batteries, e-waste), packaging returns, recalls.

### Reverse logistics network: touch points and destinations 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
Touch points: **customer (used products) → collection point → regional consolidation centre → inspection / sorting.**

| Condition after inspection | Destination |
|---|---|
| Good | **Resale** |
| Repairable | **Repair** |
| Recoverable | **Remanufacture** |
| Material recovery only | **Recycling** |
| No recovery value | **Responsible disposal** |

**Principle: "Preserve the highest possible value for the longest possible time."** 📘

### Product recovery hierarchy (value retention, highest first) ⭐⭐⭐
Reuse/resale → repair → refurbish → remanufacture → recycle (material recovery) → energy recovery → disposal (landfill). The higher the option, the more of the original materials, energy and labour embedded in the product is preserved.

### Why reverse logistics is difficult 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
| Forward logistics | Reverse logistics |
|---|---|
| Supplier → customer | Customer → supply chain |
| Predictable quantity | **Uncertain quantity** |
| Predictable destination | **Uncertain timing** |
| Known product condition | **Uncertain condition** |

**Seven challenges with examples (Lecture 8)** 📘:
| Challenge | Example 1 | Example 2 |
|---|---|---|
| **Product-condition uncertainty** | Returned smartphone: unused, lightly used, cracked or water-damaged | Returned component: repair, remanufacture or totally damaged |
| **Low return density** | Rural customers return a few units a month, so collection is unviable | Retailer collects only a few used batteries (geographic issues) |
| **Collection costs** | E-commerce returns: hundreds of items in small trips raise cost | End-of-life vehicles in remote areas: high handling and transport cost |
| **Sorting costs** | Electronic components must be separated into reuse / repair / recycle | Used packaging must be segregated by material, size, contamination |
| **Information gaps** | Returned machines: history of age, usage, failure mode unavailable | Recycling batteries: inaccurate data on age, chemistry, state of charge |
| **Lack of standardisation** | EV batteries: different manufacturers, designs, chemistries | Packaging: different suppliers, materials, dimensions |
| **Consumer participation** | Customers keep old phones instead of returning them | E-waste thrown into household waste |

### Designing collection networks: three configurations 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
| Configuration | Flow | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Decentralised** | Customer → local collection centre → regional facility | High return volumes, bulky or low-value items, need for quick local sorting |
| **B. Centralised** | Customer → collection → one central recovery facility | Low volumes, high-value items, specialised recovery technology, economies of scale |
| **C. Hybrid** | Customer → local centre → regional hub → specialised recovery facility | Large countries like India with mixed density; most common for e-waste and batteries |

**Decision factors** 📘: **volume + distance + product value + recovery technology + cost.**

### Forward + reverse = closed-loop supply chain 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
- **Forward (product flow)**: supplier → factory → DC → customer.
- **Reverse (product-return flow)**: customer → collection → recovery → factory / secondary market.
*"This is the basic architecture of a closed-loop supply chain."*

### Business value of reverse logistics ⭐⭐
Recovered value (resale, parts, materials), lower virgin-material cost, EPR compliance (batteries, e-waste, plastic packaging, end-of-life vehicles), customer loyalty (easy returns, trade-in), data on product failures for design improvement, and lower Scope 3 (purchased goods, end-of-life). EV-battery recyclers such as **Attero** and **Lohum**, and Tata Autocomp's battery work, show India building this capability under the **Battery Waste Management Rules 2022** 📘.

## Quick exam answers ⭐⭐⭐
**Q1. Define green logistics and give four practices.**
Designing logistics activities to reduce environmental impact while maintaining service and economic performance. Practices: modal shift to rail/water, route optimisation and consolidation, EV/alternative-fuel fleets, green warehousing (solar, LED), right-sized returnable packaging.

**Q2. Why is reverse logistics harder than forward logistics?**
Uncertain quantity, timing and condition; low return density; high collection and sorting cost; information gaps on product history; non-standard products; dependence on consumer participation.

**Q3. Explain how packaging affects transport emissions.**
Packaging determines cube; cube determines vehicle utilisation; utilisation determines the number of trips, and trips determine fuel and CO₂. Right-sizing and returnable packaging therefore cut emissions and cost together.

**Q4. Compare centralised and decentralised collection networks.**
Centralised sends all returns to one recovery facility (scale, specialised technology, suits low-volume high-value items, but longer transport). Decentralised sorts returns locally before regional facilities (shorter collection trips, suits high volumes and bulky items, but more facilities). Hybrid combines local centres, regional hubs and specialised recovery plants.
