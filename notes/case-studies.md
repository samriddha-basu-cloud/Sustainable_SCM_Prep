## 1. ITC eChoupal (HBS case 9-604-016, Upton & Fuller, 2003) 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
### The problem
- Agriculture was home to **72% of India's population and 60% of its workforce**.
- ITC's International Business Division (IBD) had sales of only **₹450 crore** (1998) against ₹7,701 crore for the rest of ITC; soy was two-thirds of its agri exports. Target: **₹2,000 crore by 2005**.
- Madhya Pradesh ("soyabowl") grew 4 of India's 5 million tonnes of soybean.
- The **mandi** system was inefficient: farmers hauled crops 30–50 km, waited days (Bhopal mandi had 1,700 farmers a day), sold at auction to commission agents (CAs) who could buy low and sell to ITC at market price, crude beam scales cost about 0.5% of a lot, payments were delayed, and price discovery happened only after the crop was grown and carted.
- Farmers **lost 60–70% of the potential value** of their crops; yields were a third to a quarter of global standards; risk aversion locked them in low-input, low-value farming.

### The solution (May 1999)
- A **computer kiosk in a lead farmer's home (the Sanchalak)**: PC, VSAT (in 75% of eChoupals) or phone line, **UPS and solar-battery backup**, dot-matrix printer. Set-up cost **₹1,70,000** per eChoupal plus ₹1,00,000 for people, training and software; payback expected within three years.
- Website *soyachoupal.com* in Hindi: **localised weather** (70–80 km² pockets, via IMD), **best practices** (e.g. 18-inch seed spacing instead of 9 inches), crop information and soil testing, **market prices** (mandi prices, arrivals, Chicago Board of Trade outlook), Q&A forum, news, feedback.
- **Sanchalak**: trusted literate farmer, takes a public oath, ITC logo on his house; aggregates sales and input orders; checks quality against control samples; earns **0.5% on soybean purchases** and **2–3% on inputs** (Jain of Dahod earned about ₹35,000 in a season).
- **Samyojak**: former commission agents re-employed to set up eChoupals, run warehouse hubs, handle cash and logistics; commissions of 2–5% year-round (vs 1% as CA in peak months only).
- **Hubs**: 5 processing units + 39 warehouses (44 points vs 51 big mandis); kiosk within 5 km and hub within 30 km of every farmer; **computerised weighbridge, cash on delivery, transport reimbursed**, shade, toilets, soil-testing lab, oil sold at the hub.
- By 2003: **1,695 eChoupals covering 8,400 villages, 80% of MP's soybean and wheat areas**; procurement 50% mandi / 50% eChoupal (target 20/80).
- Economics: ITC saved about **$5 per tonne** in freight and transaction costs; farmers earned about **$8 per tonne** more.
- Policy: the state amended the **APMC Act** to allow purchases outside mandis, persuaded by the transparency of published prices.
- Principles: **trust, transparency and freedom of choice** — no contract binds the farmer; information is free even if he sells at the mandi.
- Scaling: **"Roll Out, Fix It, Scale Up"**; pilots in **coffee (Karnataka, Tradersnet e-market)**, **aqua (Andhra Pradesh, traceability premium)** and **wheat (UP, quality grading)**; future use as a rural **marketing and distribution channel** (fertilisers, agri-chemicals, seeds, insurance, credit). Chairman Deveshwar: *"A commitment beyond the market."*

### Sustainability lens (Lecture 1–2 "delivery-based" case) 📘
| Pillar | Impact |
|---|---|
| Economic | Lower transaction and freight cost; better quality and less adulteration for ITC; higher farmer income; new rural sales channel |
| Social | Empowered small farmers, price transparency, fair weighing and immediate payment, digital inclusion, entrepreneurship for Sanchalaks, intermediaries re-skilled rather than eliminated |
| Environmental | Fewer wasted trips and less spoilage, best-practice inputs (right fertiliser via soil tests), solar-powered kiosks |

**Q1. Why is eChoupal an SSCM case and not just an IT project?**
It redesigned the whole value chain (village → mandi → factory) to create value for farmers, intermediaries and ITC together: information flow (price discovery, weather, practices) was digitised while physical logistics was kept with existing intermediaries. It improved all three pillars and was built on trust and inclusion (Lecture 9 calls the trade-off "technology sophistication vs inclusion").

**Q2. What roles did the Sanchalak and Samyojak play, and why keep the middlemen?**
Sanchalak: trusted village operator of the kiosk, aggregator and quality checker. Samyojak: ex-commission agent running hubs, cash and logistics. ITC kept the CAs' physical capability and local relationships but removed them from the information and price-setting flow, avoiding resistance and keeping costs low in weak infrastructure.

**Q3. Are the eChoupal savings sustainable as markets become efficient?**
Logistics savings shrink as mandis improve, so ITC built longer-term value through **traceability** (aqua premiums), **matching production to demand** (wheat quality grades) and an **electronic marketplace** (coffee Tradersnet), plus using the network as a rural distribution channel.

## 2. Tesla's battery supply chain: a growing concern (WDI case, 2022) 📘 ⭐⭐⭐
**Situation**: Tesla had about 40% of EVs on the road and claimed *"100 percent [of scrapped batteries] are recycled"*, but global recycling capacity was in its infancy. By 2030 the world faces **1.2 Mt/yr** of end-of-life batteries; only **5%** of Li-ion batteries are recycled. Tesla expects **10,000** pack replacements by 2025 and **50,000/yr** by 2030. Upstream risks: cobalt (**65% from the DRC**, child labour, *"blood diamond of batteries"*), lithium (**Lithium Triangle**, **500,000 gallons of water per tonne**), nickel (Philippines closed **23 mines** in 2017; ~20% of supply), cell concentration (**CATL ~30%**). Second-life adaptation costs **$3,500 / $6,700 / $8,000** (pack / module / cell); recycling returns about **$5,600** per battery after 2025. Options: **build** in-house recycling, **buy** a start-up, or **partner** with **Redwood Materials** (JB Straubel; 95% recovery; $3.78B) or **Li-Cycle** (>95%; $1.6B raised). ESG raters disagree (**FTSE gave Tesla zero** on environment; MSCI high). Full analysis: [Energy Sector](ch09-energy-sector.md), numbers: [P7](p5-lca-energy-numericals.md).

**Q1. Recommend build, buy or partner, with reasons.**
Partner now with Redwood or Li-Cycle under long-term offtake/equity agreements (volumes too low and technology too immature to build; recyclers need pooled scale); design packs for disassembly; plan in-house or JV capacity at the Gigafactories as volumes approach 50,000/yr. This secures critical minerals, lowers Scope 3 and conflict-mineral exposure, and gives auditable recovery data for ESG ratings.

**Q2. Is Tesla's "100% recycled" claim greenwashing?**
At risk of it: the claim lacks a stated boundary, methodology and evidence (Lecture 10's claim–metric–boundary–methodology–baseline–target–evidence test) while recycling capacity is small. Tesla should disclose recovery rates per material, volumes processed and partner audits.

**Q3. Why do ESG ratings of Tesla differ so much?**
Different boundaries and weights: FTSE looked at factory emissions (ignoring avoided tailpipe emissions), MSCI credited the product's clean-tech opportunity; Sustainalytics weighted risks such as labour and governance. Same lesson as LCA: boundaries decide results.

## 3. BRSR reports in the zip: TVS Motor and Tata Motors 📘 ⭐⭐
- **TVS Motor (FY24)**: Scope 1 + 2 = **27,130.81 t** (−5.2% vs FY23); renewable electricity **93.2%** of electricity but only **48.8%** of total energy (fuels are fossil); Scope 3 = **2.27 crore t**, **99.88%** of the footprint, about 5.41 t per vehicle sold.
- **Tata Motors CV business (FY25)**: Scope 1 + 2 = **1,75,161 t** (−20.8%); intensity 0.462 t per vehicle; Scope 3 = 14.49 crore t, of which **use of sold products = 96.3%**; Scope 3 ≈ 827 × Scope 1 + 2; assured by KPMG.
Full calculations: [CO3 Problems 4–5](p2-carbon-accounting-numericals.md).

**Q. What do these BRSR numbers tell an SCM manager?**
The plant is a tiny share of the footprint; the value chain (purchased parts and, above all, product use) dominates. Decarbonisation levers are therefore product efficiency and electrification, supplier engagement and end-of-life recovery, not only rooftop solar. Also, "93% renewable" can be true for electricity but misleading for total energy.

## 4. McKinsey Global Institute — Geopolitics and the geometry of global trade: 2026 update 📘 ⭐⭐
- Trade in 2025 **did not retrench**: global goods trade grew about **6.5%**, faster than GDP; US imports and Chinese exports hit new highs.
- **AI-related goods** (semiconductors, data-centre equipment) were about **one-third of global trade growth**, supplied by Taiwan, South Korea and Southeast Asia.
- **China became a "factory to the factories"**, exporting industrial components and capital goods to emerging economies.
- **US–China trade fell about 30%**; tariffs shifted **more than $165 billion** of trade away from the US–China corridor; the US replaced about two-thirds of the gap from other sellers; Chinese consumer-goods exporters cut prices about 8%.
- ASEAN gained; the **EU faced a double squeeze** (more Chinese imports, higher US tariffs); **India gained ground in selected sectors** (e.g. electronics/smartphones).
- Trade is **travelling shorter geopolitical distances** (reorienting towards aligned partners).
- Message: durable shifts (AI, emerging-market growth, geopolitics) plus abrupt shocks → **long-term thinking coupled with agility**.

**Q. How does this connect to SSCM strategy?**
It is the "geopolitical fragmentation" element of Lecture 11's polycrisis: supply chains must move from global-concentrated-lowest-cost to **regional, diversified, digital, circular and low-carbon** networks (JIT + JIC + JIS), with dual sourcing and scenario planning, while watching that regionalisation does not raise emissions or exclude small suppliers.
